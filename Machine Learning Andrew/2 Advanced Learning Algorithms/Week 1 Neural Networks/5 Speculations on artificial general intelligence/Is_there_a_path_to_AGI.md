# Is There a Path to Artificial General Intelligence?

## The AGI aspiration and its uncertainty

Artificial general intelligence (AGI)—an AI system able to do anything a typical human can do—is a longstanding and inspiring goal. The path toward it is not clear and may be extremely difficult. Breakthroughs might occur within decades, or AGI might take centuries or longer.

This uncertainty makes it important to distinguish real progress from unnecessary hype.

## ANI and AGI are different goals

AI includes at least two very different ideas:

| Term | Meaning | Examples and status |
|---|---|---|
| **ANI** | Artificial narrow intelligence: a system that performs one narrow task, sometimes extremely well | Smart speakers, self-driving cars, web search, and specific applications in farming or factories; ANI has made tremendous progress and creates substantial value. |
| **AGI** | Artificial general intelligence: a system that could do anything a typical human can do | A hoped-for form of general intelligence; it is unclear how much progress has been made toward it. |

Because ANI is a subset of AI, rapid ANI progress means that AI as a whole has made rapid progress. It does **not**, however, logically follow that rapid progress in AI necessarily implies rapid progress toward AGI. Confusing those claims is one source of AGI hype.

## Why simulating more neurons is not enough

Modern deep learning can simulate increasing numbers of artificial neurons, especially with faster computers and GPUs. This once encouraged the hope that simulating enough neurons might simulate a human brain and produce a highly intelligent system.

Two major obstacles make that path much less straightforward:

1. **Artificial neurons are extremely simple.** A logistic regression unit is far simpler than a biological neuron and is not an accurate model of what a neuron in the brain does.
2. **The brain remains poorly understood.** Fundamental questions remain about how a biological neuron maps its inputs to outputs. With such limited knowledge, simulating the human brain in a computer is an exceptionally difficult route to AGI.

## The one-learning-algorithm hypothesis

Some animal experiments suggest that the same piece of biological brain tissue can learn surprisingly different tasks when it receives different input data. This motivates the **one-learning-algorithm hypothesis**:

> Much of intelligence might arise from one learning algorithm, or a small handful of learning algorithms, applied to different inputs.

If such an algorithm or small set of algorithms exists and can be discovered, it might someday be possible to implement an approximation in a computer. This remains a hypothesis, not an established solution.

### Evidence from rewired sensory inputs

Experiments described in the lecture include:

- The **auditory cortex** normally receives signals from the ears and processes sound. When an animal brain is rewired so that images are sent to the auditory cortex instead, that tissue learns to see.
- The **somatosensory cortex** normally processes touch. When it receives images instead of signals from touch sensors, it can also learn to see.

These results suggest that what a brain region learns may depend greatly on the data it receives rather than on one permanently fixed task.

## Adaptation to new sensory channels

Other examples further illustrate the brain's adaptability, or **plasticity**:

- A camera mounted near a person's forehead can map a grayscale image to a grid of voltage patterns on the tongue, helping a blind person learn to interpret visual information through the tongue.
- Humans can learn a degree of echolocation by making clicking sounds and listening to how the sound reflects from nearby objects, somewhat like the sonar used by dolphins and bats.
- A belt containing a ring of buzzers can use a magnetic compass so that the buzzer pointing north always vibrates. Over time, the wearer may experience this as a direct sense of where north is rather than merely as a vibration on the waist.
- In experiments involving a third eye implanted into a frog, the brain adapts to use the additional input.

Together, these observations show that biological brains can adapt to a bewildering range of sensory inputs. They raise the question of whether a common learning mechanism underlies seeing, hearing, touching, and other forms of processing.

## A cautious conclusion

AGI remains one of the most fascinating scientific and engineering problems, but several uncertainties must be kept explicit:

- it is not known whether the brain truly relies on one algorithm or a small handful of algorithms;
- even if it does, the relevant algorithm is not known;
- discovering and approximating it would require substantial work;
- current success in ANI should not be mistaken for demonstrated progress toward human-level general intelligence.

In the shorter term, neural networks remain powerful and useful tools even without achieving AGI. They can support valuable applications without attempting to reproduce the full range of human intelligence.
